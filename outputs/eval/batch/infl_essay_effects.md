## Q1  [easy]
A national statistical office is rushing its monthly price collection. To save time, it samples only supermarkets and ignores hairdressers and taxi companies for that month. In the same month, the average shelf price of a standard loaf of bread rises by 10% compared with the reference period, while the average price of a standard adult haircut falls by 5%. Using the official approach, explain why the office’s data-collection plan is methodologically wrong and quantify how the reported picture of consumer price change would be distorted. Your answer should: (a) identify which types of outlets must be covered in monthly price collection and why, and (b) translate the given bread and haircut price changes into index values and use them to show the consequence of omitting service providers from the collection.

**Answer:** The office’s plan is methodologically wrong because monthly price collection must be carried out through price surveys that cover both shops and service providers. The recommended practice is to visit different regions and different types of outlets to record prices, explicitly including service providers, not just retail shops. Haircuts and taxi rides are services, just as bread is a good, and both goods and services form part of consumer prices used in the harmonised index of consumer prices (HICP). By skipping service providers, the office excludes a material part of the consumer price picture for that month.

Quantitatively, the index translation is straightforward: when a price is 10% higher than in the reference period, its index is 110; when a price is 5% lower, its index is 95. Therefore, bread in this month would be indexed at 110, while a standard adult haircut would be indexed at 95. If the office samples only supermarkets, it will capture the +10% for bread (110) but miss the −5% for haircuts (95). The resulting published picture will overstate overall consumer price increases because it reflects only rising goods prices and ignores falling service prices that should be part of the monthly collection. Proper coverage of both shops and service providers ensures that the combined indices across goods and services correctly represent consumer prices for the month.

## Q2  [medium]
A new analyst at a national statistical office suggests two changes: (1) outsource responsibility for choosing the items to an external vendor, and (2) keep the same set of items indefinitely. In the same reporting month, one commonly purchased item in the official set costs 10% more than in the reference period. Write an essay that, in order, explains: (a) who is responsible for selecting and maintaining the official set of items and why, (b) how that set is used in the official calculation of consumer price indices, and (c) how the 10% price rise for that item is converted into the indexed value used in the calculation. Conclude by stating what would go wrong in the index if the office adopted the analyst’s proposal.

**Answer:** (a) Responsibility for the item set rests with the national statistical office. The items “must be carefully selected to be genuinely representative of what people buy,” and the set “is reviewed each year to ensure it remains relevant; some items are removed and others put in to reflect consumer spending patterns.” Because representativeness and regular review are intrinsic to official practice, the office—not an external vendor—must define and maintain the set to meet statistical standards and ensure public comparability.

(b) Once the items in this set (the “shopping basket” of goods and services) are in place, the office records their prices each month across regions and types of outlets through price surveys. With those prices in hand, “the index is calculated” by comparing the latest prices for each item with their prices in a reference period to see how they have changed; this calculation is done item-by-item and then used to build category indices and the overall measure.

(c) The calculation converts a 10% rise relative to the reference period into an indexed value of 110 for that item (“if … the price … is 10% higher than the price in the reference period, the indexed value will be 110”). This indexed value feeds into the subsequent aggregation steps.

If the office outsourced selection or froze the set indefinitely, the basket would cease to be regularly reviewed and could stop being representative of what people actually buy. That would undermine the index: prices would still be compared to the reference period, but for an outdated or mis-specified set of items, biasing the reported picture of consumer price change away from current spending patterns.

## Q3  [hard]
A national statistical office proposes to postpone this year’s update to its official “shopping basket,” but it will continue its usual monthly price work. Write an essay that, in order, explains: (1) how the specific products whose prices are recorded each month relate to the official set of items, (2) why a yearly review is required for that official set, and (3) what exactly is changed during that review and for what purpose. Conclude by stating the consequence for representativeness if the yearly update is skipped. Your answer must follow this chain from the monthly‑priced items through the official basket and its annual review to the reason the updates are made, ending with the link to households’ spending patterns.

**Answer:** Each month, the prices actually recorded come from a defined set of products: the items that make up the official, reference shopping basket of goods and services. In other words, the items observed in monthly price collection are the same specific items that constitute the reference shopping basket used for measurement.

That reference shopping basket does not remain static. It requires a yearly review to ensure it remains relevant and genuinely representative of what people buy and the conditions under which they buy them. The annual review is therefore a required step to maintain the basket’s validity.

During this yearly review, the contents of the basket are updated: some items are removed and others are added. These updates are not arbitrary; they are carried out precisely so that the basket stays up to date with consumer spending patterns.

If the office postpones the update but continues to price the same set of items each month, the measurement will increasingly reflect an outdated basket, reducing its representativeness of current consumer spending. The consequence is that the observed prices no longer align as well with what households actually purchase, undermining the quality and relevance of the results.
